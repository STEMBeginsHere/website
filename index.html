Most students never touch the hardware side of STEM.

School computing has largely settled on software: block coding, then Python, then maybe a robotics club with a fixed kit. The physical layer underneath, where a sensor reading becomes a decision becomes a moving part, is where a lot of students would actually catch fire. It is also the part that needs equipment, a curriculum, and someone in the room who has wired a circuit before.

Equipment is the first wall

Microcontrollers, breadboards, sensors, and motors add up. A kit that sits in a closet between units is a hard line item to defend.

Then the expertise wall

Teaching physical computing well means debugging a dead circuit in front of thirty students. That is a specific skill, and it is not in most computer science certifications.

So the window closes

Students decide what they are good at long before they choose a major. If nobody puts a breadboard in front of them in middle school, most never go looking for one.

Three days. Ten hours. One thing they built themselves.

The structure is deliberately front-loaded: teach the fundamentals properly on day one, then get out of the way. By day three every student is presenting something that did not exist on Monday.

  1. Day 1 · 4 hours

    Intro to hardware engineering

    What a computer actually is, framed as input, process, output. General-purpose machines versus embedded systems. Then hands on the kit: the Raspberry Pi Pico, how a breadboard carries current, lighting an LED, and a first program. We work through every sensor and actuator, covering what it does, how it does it, and where it shows up in the world students already know.

  2. Day 2 · 4 hours

    Build your own project

    An hour of ideation, then open build time. Students design their own device and wire it themselves. Instructors circulate constantly, but the rule for our team is strict: guide students to the answer using what they already know, never hand it to them. Anyone who finishes early extends the project or starts a second one on the same board.

  3. Day 3 · 2 hours

    Showcase and awards

    Every student demonstrates their build and explains their design decisions to the room, followed by judging, awards, and a close-out. Presenting the thing turns out to matter almost as much as building it.

What they work with

Raspberry Pi Pico Breadboards Distance sensors Servos & motors LEDs & buttons Jumper wiring

It takes less than you would think.

A room, some outlets, and a group of students is close to the whole of it. We deliberately kept it that way. The Program page walks through the rest.

  • Format 3 sessions, 10 hours total
  • Grades 6 to 12, no prior experience needed
  • You bring A room with outlets, computers to code on, and an adult in the room
  • We bring Every kit, the curriculum, and the instructors
  • Cost Nothing. The program is self-funded.
