What actually happens in the room.

Three sessions, ten hours, and a kit for every student. Here is the whole thing: what gets taught, how we teach it, what we bring, and what you would need on hand.

Day by day.

The written curriculum is an outline rather than a script. Instructors are expected to read the room and adapt, because a session where thirty students move at one fixed pace is a session where a third of them quietly fall behind.

  1. Day 1 · 4 hours · Intro to hardware engineering

    From "what is a computer" to a working circuit

    We start by rebuilding the definition of a computer as anything that follows an input, process, output model, which is a bigger category than students expect. Microwaves and door locks land on the board next to laptops. From there: general-purpose machines versus embedded systems, where a microcontroller sits between them, and why a Pi Pico runs exactly one program at a time.

    Then the kit comes out. Students take apart how a breadboard actually carries current, light an LED with nothing but a battery while we troubleshoot the wiring together, and load their first program onto a Pico. We close by working through the sensors and actuators one at a time, sorting each into input or output and building something small with it.

  2. Day 2 · 4 hours · Build your own project

    An hour to decide, three to make it work

    Students spend the first hour on ideation and the rest building. This is the least scripted part of the program by design. Instructors circulate the whole time, watching for the students who are stuck on ideation rather than wiring, since those are two different kinds of stuck and they need different help.

    Anyone who finishes early gets pushed further: extend the project, or start a second one without unplugging the first, which is how students discover that one board can hold two behaviors at once.

  3. Day 3 · 2 hours · Showcase

    Demonstrate it, explain it, defend it

    Ninety minutes of showcase where every student demonstrates their build and walks the room through their design decisions, then judging, awards, and a close-out. Explaining why you chose a distance sensor over a button is where a lot of the learning consolidates.

Day 1 material adapts the Raspberry Pi Foundation's physical computing teaching module and Ada Computer Science hardware curriculum.

Nobody gets handed the answer.

This is the part of the curriculum we are strictest about, and it is written into the instructor notes on every single day.

Questions, constantly

Instructors ask rather than tell, and keep asking until the class produces the definition themselves. It is slower on paper and considerably faster in practice.

Guide, do not solve

When a student is stuck, the instruction to our team is explicit: walk them to the answer using what they already know. Fixing the circuit for them ends the problem and the learning at the same time.

Nobody left behind quietly

Small groups and constant check-ins exist so that the student who stopped understanding twenty minutes ago gets noticed, rather than waiting it out.

Who brings what.

We take care of the parts that need equipment and know-how. You take care of the room and the calendar. That is genuinely most of it.

We bring

  • A Raspberry Pi Pico kit for every student, including breadboard, sensors, motors, and wiring
  • The full curriculum and all teaching material
  • Instructors who have built this hardware themselves
  • Everything needed for the day three showcase and awards

You bring

  • A room with tables to spread out on, and outlets we can reach
  • Computers or laptops for the students, since code gets written on a regular machine and then moved onto the Pico
  • Someone from your side in the room with us
  • Three blocks of time that fit your calendar
  • A way to let students know it is happening

Still working this part out: how many students we can take at once, how many instructors we send, and our background checks and insurance. If there are boxes we need to tick before we can be in a room with your students, just tell us early and we will get them sorted.
