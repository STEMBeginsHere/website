Two Saturdays, one room full of first circuits

Our first two-day workshop, September 19 and 26, 2026. 18 students, one showcase for their families.

STEMBeginsHere·Workshop recap

We have spent a long time thinking about what the first hour of hardware should feel like for a student who has never touched it. On two Saturdays in September we found out, at Maricopa Institute of Technology, with 18 students in grades 6 through 12 from Gilbert, Chandler, and Phoenix-area schools.

This was our first time running the program as a two-day workshop: one day to learn the parts, and one day to build something of your own and show it to the people you brought with you.

Day one: what a computer actually is

We started where every hardware engineer starts: what a computer is underneath the screen. Then everyone got their hands on the kit, which is a Raspberry Pi Pico microcontroller, a breadboard, jumper wires, LEDs, and a set of sensors.

Each new part came with the same three questions: what is it, how does it work, and why would anyone use it? A tilt switch, for example, is a metal ball rolling downhill, wired up. Tip it past an angle and the ball closes the circuit. Once a student sees that, they start spotting it everywhere, from phones that know which way is up to alarms that trip when something falls over.

At the end of the first day, one student summed it up: "I learned how to use the breadboard and how to do coding. I do want to come back next Saturday."

The part where it doesn't work yet

Most of day one looked like this: heads down, wires in, something not lighting up, and then something lighting up. Our instructors guide rather than solve. That's slower, and it's the point. A student who finds their own loose wire learns more than a student who watches someone else fix it.

Day two: build it, then explain it

A week later, students came back to design and wire their own projects. Mentors circulated and asked questions like "what do you expect this pin to do?" instead of pointing at the answer.

The best moment of any workshop is when a student turns the laptop around and says, "watch this."

The workshop ended with a showcase. Families came in, and students walked them through what they built, how it works, and what they would change next time. Explaining your own circuit to someone who didn't build it is harder than building it, which is exactly why we end every workshop this way.

Graduation day

We closed in the auditorium with a certificate ceremony. Families filled the seats, and one by one the students walked up to receive their certificates.

In their own words

At the ceremony, each student shared what they learned or what they want to build next. A few of our favorites:

"When you turn them on, in Morse code, they can flash SOS and you can leave a trail behind, just in case you get lost in the woods."

"My goal was to learn how to do advanced coding, and eventually make that work, like I did."

"Probably learn how to code and work for NASA. That would be really fun."

What's next

This workshop showed us that the two-day format works: enough time to learn the fundamentals properly, and enough time to build something original. We are now scheduling one-day and two-day workshops with schools across Arizona, and we are growing the team of volunteers and club leaders who make that possible.

  • Schools: book a workshop for your students, free.
  • Volunteers: help run the next one.
  • Supporters: fund kits so more students get a turn.
